There is a cheap way to argue about the Peak Cluster carbon capture pipeline, and there is an honest way.

The cheap way is to pick one column and pretend the other one is blank.

Opponents can say: this is a dangerous industrial pipeline forced through Wirral and Cheshire so Derbyshire and Staffordshire can send their problem to our coast. Supporters can say: this is essential climate infrastructure for hard-to-decarbonise cement and lime, and objectors are standing in the way of net zero.

Both sentences contain truth. Neither is enough.

The Down the Banksy rule is harder: **No is not free. Yes is not free either. Price both columns.** Inaction is a choice with costs, and the work is to make the invoice visible. The tool is a ledger, not an oracle: it does not force a yes, but it forbids a free no.

## The proposition

The decision is whether the UK should permit the Peak Cluster carbon capture and storage infrastructure, including a new underground onshore CO₂ pipeline from cement and lime plants in Derbyshire and Staffordshire to a coastal above-ground installation on the Wirral, then onward to offshore storage beneath the East Irish Sea.

The [project description](https://peakcluster.co.uk/the-project/) proposes capture at cement and lime plants, an underground pipeline and storage at Morecambe Net Zero beneath the East Irish Sea. Peak Cluster says the onshore project requires a Development Consent Order, with the Secretary of State making the final planning decision. That describes a consent process, not an approval. See its [planning explanation](https://peakcluster.co.uk/your-questions-answered/).

## The stated benefit

The stated benefit is serious. Cement and lime are not decorative extras in a modern economy. The question is how to supply useful materials with much lower emissions, not whether anyone can wish the need for them away.

The [Climate Change Committee’s Seventh Carbon Budget](https://www.theccc.org.uk/publication/the-seventh-carbon-budget/) identifies cement and lime process emissions as difficult to abate through fuel switching. Its pathway relies heavily on CCS but also on resource efficiency, clinker substitution and using less concrete. It recognises innovative cements that could reduce process emissions and, with development, reduce reliance on CCS. That supports a role for capture; it does not establish that this particular route is the only answer.

The developer forecasts capture of over 3 million tonnes of CO₂ a year. Its [economic case](https://peakcluster.co.uk/the-project/) claims around £5 billion of investment with Morecambe Net Zero, £1.8 billion in economic value, about 2,000 existing jobs safeguarded and 1,500 construction roles. The [FAQ](https://peakcluster.co.uk/your-questions-answered/) puts the broader combined jobs claim above 13,000 by 2050. These are projected benefits with different scopes, not delivered results or numbers to add together.

The date needs correcting: the [March 2026 pipeline factsheet](https://peakcluster-consultation.co.uk/files/Peak%20Cluster%20Factsheet_2_About%20the%20pipeline_March%202026.pdf) anticipates construction from 2029 and operations in **2032**. A [government funding description](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/industrial-energy-transformation-fund-ietf-competition-winners/ietf-phase-3-spring-2024-competition-winners) also uses 2032. The developer’s FAQ still contains an older 2030 claim. This article uses the dated 2032 programme as a proposal, not a guaranteed start.

That is the “Yes” column’s strongest case. It is not nothing. It cannot be dismissed as corporate greenwash without doing the work.

## The cost of acting: the visible bill

Now price the other side.

The [March pipeline factsheet](https://peakcluster-consultation.co.uk/files/Peak%20Cluster%20Factsheet_2_About%20the%20pipeline_March%202026.pdf) describes an onshore line around 200 km long, up to 36 inches (91.4 cm) in diameter. Its [above-ground infrastructure factsheet](https://peakcluster-consultation.co.uk/files/Peak%20Cluster_Factsheet_3_Above%20ground%20infrastructure_March%202026.pdf) gives a maximum coastal installation footprint of 300 m by 180 m, most structures no higher than 15 m and a vent stack up to 50 m. These are proposed dimensions, not a completed design. The coastal stack is for maintenance or exceptional releases, not continuous venting or emptying the whole pipeline.

The pipeline factsheet proposes mainly open-trench construction, with a typical working width of 30–40 m and about 50 m between the coastal installation and landfall. Trenchless methods may be used at particular crossings. The construction footprint is not the same thing as the wider corridor being consulted on.

That means the cost of “yes” is not only theoretical. It includes the prospect of traffic, noise, construction disturbance, land-use restrictions and uncertainty. How much, where, for how long and with what remedy are questions for the assessment, not figures to invent.

The local environmental questions have official substance. The [Planning Inspectorate’s February 2026 scoping opinion and consultation responses](https://nsip-documents.planninginspectorate.gov.uk/published-documents/EN0710001-000018-EN0710001%20-%20EIA%20Scoping%20Opinion.pdf) raise protected habitats around the North Wirral coast, Meols Meadow SSSI, flood risk associated with the River Birket and possible interaction with Hoole Bank Tar Lagoon. These are issues requiring assessment, not proof that each feared impact will occur.

The safety issue must be stated precisely. [HSE’s current hazard guidance](https://www.hse.gov.uk/carboncapture/major-hazard.htm) describes high-pressure CO₂ release hazards, including cold low-lying clouds, damage from rapid decompression and asphyxiation. Regulation is not a finding that the risk is zero.

The legal position is no longer just an announcement: [regulation 4 of SI 2026/185](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2026/185/pdfs/uksi_20260185_en.pdf), in force from 6 April 2026, adds CO₂ in any fluid state, including predominantly CO₂ mixtures, used exclusively for CCUS and associated activities to the dangerous-fluid schedule of the Pipelines Safety Regulations. That is the relevant change. HSE’s older pipeline guidance still carries the previous classification; it should not be used to deny the amendment.

So, the honest opposition is not: “They are bringing toxic waste to the beach.”

The honest opposition is: They are asking a local population to host major accident hazard infrastructure, ecological disruption, construction damage, and permanent above-ground plant in order to solve a national industrial emissions problem. That may or may not be justified — but it must not be waved through as if the local bill is imaginary.

## The cost of not acting: the hidden bill

Now price “no.”

Rejecting this proposal would avoid the construction and operating impacts of this proposal in this corridor. That matters. It does not decide what happens to the underlying demand for materials or their emissions.

But refusal does not, by itself, make cement and lime clean. The replacement case must compare actual alternatives: process changes, lower material demand, other capture arrangements, another route, and the option of continued emissions. Different combinations have different timescales and costs.

So, the invoice moves.

If domestic production continues without another way of reducing its emissions, some intended savings could be delayed or lost. If production moves, imports could shift emissions and employment elsewhere. Those are scenarios to investigate, not a prediction that rejecting one pipeline inevitably closes the industry. Conversely, a different process or route might deliver a better result. The counterfactual has to be demonstrated, not awarded to either side by rhetoric.

That is the “No” column’s hardest truth. Local refusal can be morally legitimate. But it is not automatically morally clean.

## The displacement

The Peak Cluster dispute is so explosive because the benefit and the burden do not land in the same place.

The emissions come from cement and lime plants in Derbyshire and Staffordshire. The pipeline route crosses other communities. The coastal infrastructure lands on the Wirral. The storage is offshore. The climate benefit is national and global. The construction harm is local and concentrated.

That is the displacement problem: a national benefit can depend on concentrated local burdens. The local case cannot be answered merely by naming the global benefit, and the local refusal cannot be evaluated without asking what happens elsewhere.

Could another network or storage arrangement provide a better alternative? That needs technical evidence about capacity, timing and connections. Comparing two headline storage capacities does not, by itself, prove that a shared solution is impossible.

## The disowned bill

Each side has a temptation.

The developers’ temptation is to talk as if safety regulation, biodiversity net gain, consultation and restoration make the local cost disappear. They do not. Even if every mitigation works, a mitigated cost is still a cost.

The opponents’ temptation is to talk as if refusing the pipeline returns the world to innocence. It does not. A refused pipeline still leaves cement and lime emissions somewhere in the ledger. A refused route still leaves the UK needing materials. A refused project still raises the question: what replaces it, when, at what scale, and whose land, jobs, climate and infrastructure pay instead?

That is why the question cannot be “Are you for or against the pipeline?”

The question is: whose invoice are you reading, and whose are you refusing to open?

## The asymmetry check

The evidence standard must be the same on both sides.

If developers want local people to trust them, they need more than reassurance. They need route clarity, emergency planning, flood-risk disclosure, ecological mitigation that is local and enforceable, compensation mechanisms that reach tenant farmers and affected residents, and a serious answer to why this corridor, why this coast, and why these communities.

But if opponents want the moral authority of refusal, they also need more than fear. They need to price the alternative. Not the imaginary alternative where a perfect zero-carbon cement system appears on demand. The real alternative: continuing emissions, delayed decarbonisation, imports, industrial decline, or another route through another community.

The latest [consultation update](https://peakcluster.co.uk/your-questions-answered/), checked on 25 October 2026, describes location-focused consultation in Staffordshire, Derbyshire and Cheshire in November 2026, followed by Wirral in January 2027. A fuller phase 2 consultation is planned for summer 2027. Those plans remain subject to change. The route is being refined: uncertainty is a reason to scrutinise the evidence, not permission for either blind trust or invented certainty.

## The honest ledger

Here is the honest version.

Yes to Peak Cluster could contribute to industrial decarbonisation and protect productive capacity, if capture, transport and storage deliver the claimed performance. That is a conditional case, not a completed outcome.

But yes also asks communities to host the pipeline and associated works. Proposed mitigation must be evaluated, costed and made enforceable; it cannot be counted as if it had already succeeded.

No protects the local corridor from this project. It may be the right answer if the route is unsafe, the mitigation is weak, the compensation is unjust, the ecological damage is unacceptable, or the developers cannot prove their case.

But no does not erase the materials problem. Its bill depends on what replaces the proposal, when it can work, what emissions remain and who bears the consequences. That may favour another solution. It may reveal a substantial cost of delay. Neither outcome can be assumed for free.

So, the serious demand is not “approve it.”

The serious demand is this:

Price both columns. Publish both invoices. Name every some-other-fucker before the Secretary of State signs anything.

If Peak Cluster proceeds, my minimum terms would be transparent route evidence, public emergency-response information, independent safety scrutiny, enforceable local ecological mitigation, flood-risk evidence for the coastal infrastructure, fair treatment of people whose land and livelihoods are disrupted, and support for lower-carbon material alternatives. Those are demands in this argument, not a claim that every measure is already promised or legally secured.

Down the Banksy’s wall text writes itself:

> Planning objection received.  
> Invoice required.  
> Yes column.  
> No column.  
> No blank spaces.
