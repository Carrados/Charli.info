*Madison McDonnell, Access Provocateur, on the unnamed button that turned a £2,000 regular into a lukewarm critic.*

I’ve said it before and I’ll keep saying it: consent is sexy. Ask first, make it clear, and take “not now” for an answer. Apparently, that’s too much to expect from a brunch app.

My friend [Charlotte Joanne](https://blind-ant.com/pieces/it-wasnt-me/), the Blind Ant woman, has spent about £2,000 on the Brunch Café app in a year. That isn’t a habit. That’s a relationship. She loves the food, she loves the people, and as far as the app is concerned, she thinks they’re three out of five. Distinctly meh. The café noticed and rang her, worried, to ask what they’d done wrong.

They hadn’t done anything wrong. Neither had she. The button had.

## The button with no name

Charlotte Joanne is blind and uses VoiceOver on her iPhone. When she opens the app, VoiceOver reads out “How was your order”, followed by a control with no label at all. No “Rate”. No “Skip”. No “Not now”. Just a button that won’t tell you its name, which in my experience is never a good start to anything.

She double-taps it, because that’s how you get past a pop-up. The prompt disappears and she’s straight into the app, ready to order. As far as she knows, she’s dismissed it. Meanwhile, at the café’s end, another three-star review arrives with her name on it.

Nobody has yet proven exactly how one becomes the other. Maybe three stars is pre-selected. Maybe closing the prompt counts as voting. Maybe she has rated the same eggs Benedict every time she has opened the app. That’s for the people who built it to find out. But the shape of the problem is clear enough: an action happened in her name that she couldn’t see, couldn’t understand and never agreed to.

## A tap is not a yes

This is the bit I want the accessibility world to take personally. We talk about labels and roles as if they’re housekeeping. Tidy your controls, pass your audit, move on. They’re not housekeeping. They’re how a person knows what they’re agreeing to.

If a screen reader user can’t tell what a control does, then pressing it isn’t a choice. It’s a guess. And when the guess publishes an opinion, you haven’t collected feedback. You’ve made it up and signed her name to it.

That’s consent by double-tap. It’s not consent.

## Everybody loses

The mad thing is that nobody in this story wins. Charlotte Joanne gets misquoted. The café gets its rating dragged down by one of its best customers, with her money still warm in the till. And the app gets data that says the opposite of the truth. Two thousand pounds of loyalty, filed as a shrug.

Worse, if this review prompt is a shared component, and these things usually are, it’s sitting inside other restaurant apps doing the same thing to other blind regulars. Somewhere, a small café is wondering why its most devoted customers seem so unimpressed.

Our resident sceptic at The Blind AI, [Eval Noah Curare](https://charli.info/charlipedia/eval-noah-curare/), had views. He always does.

> *“This is the purest eval I have ever seen. No rubric. No preference data. No human in the loop, just a human’s thumb in the loop, which is cheaper. One blind woman, one nameless button, one pre-chewed vibe: three stars. Ship it! Benchmark it! Put it on a slide at a conference and call it ground truth! The café rated by the app, the app rated by the café, and nobody asked the thumb. I’ve been a parrot for forty years and even I only say what I mean. Polly wants a labelled button. Polly wants a labelled button. SQUAWK. Three stars.”*

Thank you, Eval. Go and lie down.

## What she asked for

Charlotte Joanne didn’t write a rant. She wrote the café a very polite email (which bounced, naturally; the food always arrives, the complaint couldn’t find the door) asking them to take it to the app makers and get them to:

- test the review prompt on a real iPhone with VoiceOver on, and record exactly what it announces;

- explain how a review gets submitted, including whether dismissing counts and whether a rating is pre-selected;

- give every control a proper name and role, and add a “Not now” that genuinely submits nothing;

- remove the reviews she never meant, without quietly swapping them for five stars, which would just be a nicer lie;

- say whether blind people test their releases, and whether other apps share the same prompt.

That list is the whole fix. None of it is hard. All of it is overdue.

## The sexy bit

People ask me why I bother making accessibility sexy. This is why. Accessibility isn’t a compliance checkbox. It’s about whether you’re in control of what happens in your name, whether you’re signing for a parcel, buying a bra or rating a brunch.

A woman who has spent two grand on eggs knows exactly what she thinks of them. Let her say it herself.

Five stars from her, for the record. The button can earn its own.
