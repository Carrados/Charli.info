## About the Piece

This is my account of why Blind Ant has the name it has, and of how a piece about a game of Go met a week in mathematics. It is an essay about noticing patterns before they become the news.

## The account

People sometimes ask whether Blind Ant is a typo. It isn't. It's a homage.

In William Gibson's Blue Ant trilogy, Blue Ant is the agency run by Hubertus Bigend, a man who collects people who can see what is coming. The first book is called *[Pattern Recognition](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/23650/pattern-recognition-by-william-gibson/9780141904467)*. Its heroine, Cayce Pollard, has a gift that is half talent and half affliction: she can tell, before anyone else, which shapes in the noise are about to matter. Brands hurt her. Bigend pays her to look at a logo and say whether it will work.

Blind Ant is that agency with the lights off. Our logo has never been drawn. Nobody has ever seen it. It exists only as described, and that is the point. Many army ants are blind, and the colony still finds its way, by trail and touch and the pattern laid down by everything that went before. That is the method here. No vision. Plenty of pattern.

Which brings me to this week.

## Eight months in a drawer

On 15 March this year, the tenth anniversary of the last game of [AlphaGo](https://deepmind.google/technologies/alphago/)'s match against Lee Sedol, a piece called *[The Stories Were True](https://carrados.com/blind-ai/the-stories-were-true/)* was written. It is about Move 78: the wedge Lee Sedol played in game four, a move AlphaGo had priced at one in ten thousand. It broke the machine and won him the only game a human ever took from it. He still lost the match 4–1.

The piece sat unpublished for eight months. On Thursday 19 November it went out on The Blind AI, at Carrados.

On Friday 20 November, mathematics played its own game five.

In the morning, [Levent Alpöge](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/anthropic-mathematician-claims-to-prove-riemann-hypothesis-most-famous-problem-in-all-of-math/), a mathematician at Anthropic, said that working with Claude he believes he has proved the Riemann hypothesis, and the generalised version with it. There is no readable paper. There are about seventeen million lines of Lean, a language a computer can check line by line, and [independent mathematicians have compiled it cleanly](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-ai-riemann-hypothesis-proof-is-almost-certainly-true-independent-mathematicians-attest/).

Hours later, Ravi Vakil, president of the American Mathematical Society, put out what he called [a hurried announcement](https://math216.wordpress.com/2026/11/20/hurried-announcement-alessandro-masullo-grh/). Alessandro Masullo has a proposed proof of the generalised Riemann hypothesis: a 940-page manuscript, *[The Music of the Primes](https://math216.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/the-music-of-the-primes.pdf)*, twelve years of work, read closely for months by Brian Conrad. All human. No AI. His hand had been forced.

Neither proof is settled yet. Both could turn out to be right; two true proofs never contradict each other. But read Thursday's piece again with Friday in mind.

*“The grace note was real. The war was over. Both things were true simultaneously.”*

Twelve years alone on the hardest problem in number theory, finishing within hours of a machine that took six weeks. If that isn't Move 78, I don't know what is.

## Not psychic

It would be easy to call the timing psychic. It isn't. It's pattern recognition.

Nobody could have known the day. But anyone watching the trail could see the shape. In August, [Claude raised a long-standing bound on the zeros of the Riemann zeta function](https://www.anthropic.com/research/riemann-zeta). In October, [OpenAI dropped hundreds of results at once, among them a proof of the quasi-Riemann hypothesis](https://www.quantamagazine.org/ai-proved-the-quasi-riemann-hypothesis-what-happens-now-20261019/). In November, the formal proofs started arriving faster than people could read them. The match was nearly over. A piece about the last human move was always going to be needed soon.

The same thing happened in a smaller way this week. A podcast host described the Star Trek computer, the one you simply talk to, as where all of this is heading. Blind Ant has been saying that for nearly a year. That isn't foresight either. Blind people have been talking to computers for forty years. We noticed the Star Trek computer arriving because it is the interface we have always used. The future comes unevenly, and some of us live in the early bits because we need them first.

That is what Blind Ant is for. Cayce Pollard's gift is useless unless someone writes it down before it happens. So, we write it down: as design fiction, as stories set a little ahead, as the pattern filed before it becomes the news.

The ants can't see. They still get there first.
