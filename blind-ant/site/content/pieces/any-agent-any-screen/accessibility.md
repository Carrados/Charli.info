*A Future History, part two. The same eighteen months, as one blind woman lives them.*

*Design fiction, written 18 November 2026.*

## November 2026: She Was Already There

Maya Quinn has been blind since birth. She has never seen a website.

She has heard thousands of them, one element at a time, through a screen reader. Heading. Link. Button. Unlabelled button. Image. Image. Image.

When the personal agents arrive, the commentators call it the beginning of voice-first computing.

Maya has been voice-first all her life.

What changes is not the voice. It is that, for the first time, she can ask for the result instead of the route. Her agent reads her bank statement and tells her what has changed. It does not tell her where the button is.

But the agent still works through the same websites she does. When her energy supplier redesigns its account page, the agent stumbles exactly where she used to.

The web was built to be looked at. Her agent is just a faster way of not looking.

## April 2027: Built Her Way, By Accident

The first dedicated agent devices go on sale. No screen worth mentioning. Speech in, speech out.

The reviewers find it strange. Maya finds it ordinary, and then, slowly, remarkable.

For the first time in her life, a mainstream device has been designed around the way she already uses technology. Not adapted for her. Not given an accessibility mode. Simply built for talking.

It does not last.

The moment a task needs detail, the device says the same thing it says to everyone: *I've sent that to your phone.*

The companion app is a visual app. Some of its buttons are labelled. The holiday itinerary is a picture of a map.

The device built her way hands her back to an interface that was not.

## June 2027: The End of the Form

Maya's bank opens a Domain Expert to authorised agents.

She asks her agent to move her savings into a fixed-rate account. It takes forty seconds. There is no form, no date picker, no CAPTCHA, no session that times out while she is tabbing to the submit button.

She realises she has been bracing for that form for twenty years.

Then her agent tries her council.

The council's Domain Expert answers every question about bin collections, except the new garden waste charge. That exists only as a scanned leaflet: a photograph of words, with no words behind it.

The agent cannot read it. Neither can Maya. Neither, it turns out, can anyone else's agent.

For the first time, sighted people's agents fail exactly where she always has. The information was lost before anyone came looking for it.

The council fixes it within a week. Thirty years of her complaints never managed that.

## September 2027: Everyone Wants a Screen

The voice-first devices have a problem. People want to see what their agents are talking about.

Maya does not want to see anything. But she does want to read.

Speech is fine for a bus time. It is no good for checking the small print of a mortgage offer, line by line, at her own pace. For that she uses her braille display: forty cells of raised pins on her desk.

When the manufacturers start passing agent interfaces from one device to another, Maya's agent asks a question none of the launch demonstrations considered.

Is a braille display a screen?

In the first systems, it is not. A screen is something with pixels.

A small group of developers, most of them blind, publish a description of a braille display as a surface an agent can borrow: forty cells, one line, read by touch, private by default.

It is the first time the question *what can you show?* has an answer that does not involve light.

## January 2028: The Seat-Back Screen

Maya takes the train to Edinburgh. Four and a half hours. There is a screen in the back of the seat in front of her, as there has been on every journey she has ever made.

She has never used one. They are touchscreens, silent, and laid out for eyes.

This time her agent says: *The seat-back display is offering itself. It can speak through your earphones. Do you want it?*

She says yes.

The screen does not speak to her the way the screen speaks to the man across the aisle. It does not have to. Her agent asks it for the journey, the stops, the delay at Newcastle and the menu in the buffet car, and gives them to her in the order she would have asked for them.

The man across the aisle, who has low vision, has the same screen showing the same journey in text so large it fills the display with four words at a time.

Two people. One kind of screen. Two completely different interfaces, neither of them an accessibility mode.

When she gets off at Waverley, the screen forgets her.

## February 2028: The Marker She Cannot See

Maya hears about the Shotgun Incident on the radio. Hundreds of public screens, eleven minutes, a message nobody authorised.

The manufacturers promise a fix. Every borrowed screen will show a trusted marker in its corner, drawn by the device itself, so people can always tell which agent is speaking.

Maya laughs out loud in her kitchen.

A mark in the corner of a screen is exactly the kind of security she has never been able to check. The padlock in the address bar. The blue tick. The logo that tells you the email really is from your bank.

If a spoofed screen can speak to her through her agent, a picture of a padlock protects everyone except her.

She writes to the standards group. So do several thousand other people. Their point is simple: trust that only works for the eyes is not trust. It is a visual design.

The argument that follows is the first time the people building the protocol have to answer a blind user before they ship rather than after.

## March 2028: Trust in Every Sense

The universal interface protocol is published.

Most of the coverage is about the coalition behind it and the end of the companion app. Maya reads the specification itself, with her agent's help, section by section.

Two clauses matter to her.

The first says that a device announcing itself must say what it can do in every sense it has: light, sound, touch. A braille display is a surface. So is a speaker. So is a watch that can only vibrate.

The second says that the trusted marker must reach the person through the sense they are using. A chime only the device can make. A pattern of vibration. A word on a braille cell that no agent can draw.

Neither clause says *accessibility*. They do not need to.

For the first time, the thing she needs is not an extra. It is how the system works.

## Easter 2028: The Interface Comes to Her

Maya's new agent device arrives on Good Friday. It is small, it talks, and it does not need an app.

On Easter Sunday her nephew asks her to help plan a family holiday. The agent puts the options on the television for everyone else: photographs, prices, a map. It reads the same options to Maya through her earphones, and sends the cancellation terms to her braille display, because she always reads the small print.

Nobody has made anything accessible. Nobody has had to.

For thirty years Maya learned interfaces that were designed for someone else. She learned where the buttons were, which ones had no names, which pages would break after the next redesign.

Now she asks for what she wants.

And the world decides how to tell her.
