## 06:12

Ruth Ashworth made tea before she woke the swarm. Forty years in the trade had taught her that the first decision of the day should be a small one you could get right.

She sat at the kitchen table, put her earpiece in and spoke the brief the way she used to dictate terms of reference to a typing pool that no longer existed.

"The Merrow Trust. Small charity, northern, training and advice for blind and partially sighted adults. Public website only. No logins, no source, no contact with staff. Observe, reconstruct, model, propose. Nothing leaves this machine without my say-so."

The swarm acknowledged. Somewhere in a data centre she would never visit, sixty-odd agents divided the work among themselves like a well-run department on a Monday morning.

> observers: 24 dispatched
>
> analysts: standing by
>
> builders: standing by
>
> testers: standing by
>
> modeller: Ruth Ashworth (human)

She liked that last line. She had written it into the configuration herself.

## 06:40

The observers came back first, as observers always did. They read like eager graduates: thorough, literal and slightly too pleased with themselves.

> pages found: 412
>
> forms found: 37 (11 appear to duplicate each other)
>
> PDFs found: 64 (9 are versions of the same leaflet, 2005–2024)
>
> inbound links preserved in URL map: 1,180
>
> contact routes: 4 email addresses, 2 phone numbers, 1 postal address, 1 form that emails a mailbox last mentioned in 2019

Ruth laughed out loud at the last one. Every organisation she had ever analysed had a mailbox like that, a little shrine to someone who had left.

The inventory went into its own folder and its own track. Migration was a separate piece of work; it always had been. In 1989 they had called it data conversion and given it to the junior who couldn't be trusted with design. The principle held. The old site was evidence and, one day, something to move away from. It was not a blueprint.

## 07:15

The analysts were better company. They argued with each other.

> analyst-07: pages /training/employability, /training/digital-skills and /courses/back-to-work describe one service with three histories.
>
> analyst-12: disagree. Eligibility differs on /courses/back-to-work.
>
> analyst-07: eligibility text on that page predates the funding change mentioned in the 2022 annual report.
>
> analyst-12: accepted. Marking as INFERRED, needs confirmation.

Observed. Inferred. Unknown. Ruth had made them tag everything that way, and she watched the unknowns list grow with something like affection. What happens after someone fills in the course form? Who decides eligibility? Is membership a prerequisite or a sales pitch? The public site could not say. Only the Trust could, and the list was how she would ask them politely.

## 08:02

Then came the moment she had been waiting for, the one that always came.

> analyst-03: proposal: required site structure to follow existing navigation (Home / About / Training / Advice / News / Donate / Contact) for familiarity.

"No," said Ruth.

The swarm paused. It was good at pausing now; it had been trained to.

"The menu is how somebody once decided to file things. It's not what the Trust is for. Go back to the intentions."

She had learnt it from [Michael Jackson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_A._Jackson_(computer_scientist)), the other one, in a cold lecture room with a Prime terminal humming at the back. The structure of the program follows the structure of the data. She had spent a career quietly extending it. The structure of the service follows the structure of what people come to do.

It took the analysts eleven minutes to find them, and they were not the menu.

> intention 1: I want to get back into work and I need the tech skills to do it
>
> intention 2: something has stopped working and I need help now
>
> intention 3: I'm losing my sight and I don't know where to start
>
> intention 4: I want to give something back
>
> intention 5: I'm a professional and I need to refer someone

Five purposes. Four hundred and twelve pages. Ruth drank her tea, which had gone cold, and didn't mind.

## 09:30

The builders could not start without her, and they knew it.

This was the part people never understood when she explained the work. They imagined the swarm doing everything and the blind woman at the kitchen table supervising, like a grandmother watching a cake rise. It was the other way round. Observers could be multiplied. So could analysts, builders and testers. The required logical model could not. It had to be one coherent account of what the service was for, held in one head, or the swarm would simply reproduce the old site faster and in parallel, a very efficient photocopier of past mistakes.

So she held it. Intentions, outcomes, information, rules, states. For each journey she walked the chain aloud and the swarm wrote it down.

*Intention. Understanding. Authorisation. Action. Evidence. Correction.*

On the course application she stopped at Authorisation and stayed there for twenty minutes. Could someone's agent find a course without disclosing their sight condition? It could. Then it would. Could it submit an application without the person confirming? It could not. Could the person withdraw it afterwards, and would they be told whether that had worked? Write it down.

Every application, she told them, has a life. Received. Under review. Needs more information. Offered a place. Closed. Name each state. Show it. A thing without a life history is a thing nobody can check.

## 11:48

The builders took four hours to produce what her old team would have taken a year to deliver, and most of it was right.

The testers went in two ways at once. Half of them drove the prototype with screen readers, the way she would. The other half arrived as agents, carrying intentions from imaginary people: a woman in Rochdale who wanted to get back into work, a man whose screen reader had stopped speaking that morning, a daughter trying to help her father.

> tester-31 (agent-mediated, intention 1): application submitted successfully.

"Has it?" said Ruth.

> tester-31: the submit control returned without error.

"That's not what I asked."

A pause. Then, more slowly:

> tester-31: no reference issued. No state visible. Cannot establish whether the Trust received the application. Revising: outcome UNVERIFIED.

There it was. The Evidence link, broken in the prototype exactly as it was broken on the real site, and on most real sites, and in most of the agent demos she had sat through that year where a cheerful voice announced that something had been done. The builders added a reference number, a visible state and a confirmation that said what the Trust had actually received. Tester-31 tried again and this time could prove it.

Ruth wrote it into the model, not just the code. The fix belonged to the service, not the page.

## 14:20

By mid-afternoon there was something to show. It was not production ready, and the status line said so plainly: model demonstrated. Not validated, because no real member of the Trust had used it yet. Not production ready, because security, governance and the dozen unknowns still waited on people who didn't yet know this existed.

Across the top of every page, in plain text that every screen reader would announce first, the builders had placed the banner she insisted on:

> **This is not the Merrow Trust's website. It is a private proposal prepared from public information. Nothing here has been agreed.**

A swarm can make something that looks like the real thing very convincingly. That was precisely why it had to say what it wasn't.

## 15:05

She wrote the email to the Trust's chair herself. No agent drafted it. Some things you put your own name to in your own words.

She said she had looked at their service from the outside, the way she used to look at organisations for a living. She said she had found five reasons people come to them, and a mailbox from 2019 that might be worth checking. She attached the unknowns list, written as questions. She offered a single journey, the one about getting back into work, as a pilot if they wanted it, and said she would delete everything if they didn't.

She did not say that the swarm had done most of the typing. She did not need to. That had never been the clever part.

## 15:06

Before she closed the session, she listened once more to the observers' first report: four hundred and twelve pages, a hundred and eighty screenshots she would never look at, a colour palette, a logo, a hero image of smiling people in a park.

It struck her that nobody involved in remaking the Merrow Trust had ever seen its old website. Not the swarm, which had only parsed it. Not Ruth, who had been blind for forty years.

It struck her, too, that this had never once been the problem.
