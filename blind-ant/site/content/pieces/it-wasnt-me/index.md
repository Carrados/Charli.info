## About the Piece

This is my account of a button with no name. It sits on the review prompt of a brunch app I use with VoiceOver, and when I double-tap it to get past the pop-up, something seems to leave the building with my name on it. I have set out what I heard, what I did, what I have asked for, and what nobody has yet worked out.

## The account

*I spent £2,000 on brunch and got three stars for my trouble. Her own three stars, apparently.* Let’s get the number out of the way, because it does a lot of work in this story. In my first year with the Brunch Café app, I spent around £2,000. Two thousand pounds. On brunch. Delivered.

Depending on who you ask, that is either the mark of a fiercely loyal customer or something a financial adviser would want a quiet word about. I prefer the first reading. I’m not so much a regular as a minor shareholder who takes her dividends in hollandaise.

So, imagine my delight when the café got in touch, worried, to ask what was wrong. They’d been getting three-star reviews from me. Three stars. The Travelodge of ratings. The rating that means “it was food, and I ate it.” Two grand of devotion, summed up in a shrug.

Here’s the thing. It wasn’t me.

Or rather, it was me in the way the dog eating your homework is the dog’s doing. My thumb was technically involved. Nobody asked my thumb’s opinion.

### What actually happens

I’m blind, and I use VoiceOver, the screen reader built into my iPhone. When I open the app, VoiceOver says “How was your order” and then offers me a button with no name. Not “Rate.” Not “Skip.” Just a button, standing there like a man at a bus stop who won’t tell you where he’s going.

I double-tap it, because that’s how you get past things. The prompt goes away and I’m in, logged in, ready to order more eggs. From where I’m sitting, I’ve closed a pop-up.

From where the café is sitting, I’ve just told the world they’re fine. Middling. Adequate. Three stars.

Nobody has yet worked out exactly how one turns into the other. Is three stars pre-selected? Does dismissing count as voting? Have I been rating the same breakfast every time I open the app, like some lukewarm Groundhog Day? I can tell you what VoiceOver said and what I did. The café can tell you which reviews arrived. The bit in the middle belongs to the people who built the app, and that’s where I’ve asked them to look.

### The good news about the £2,000

It turns out being a big spender is quite useful in a dispute. It’s the one thing in my letter nobody can argue with. When a customer who has spent that much says she’s happy, three stars stops looking like an opinion and starts looking like a bug. The money is evidence. Two thousand pounds of character reference.

And, to be fair, the ordering side of the app works beautifully with VoiceOver, which is how I managed to spend it all. Credit where it’s due: if the whole thing had been inaccessible, I’d be £2,000 richer and a great deal less well fed.

### The bad news about the £2,000

It bought me precisely nothing at the moment it mattered. Two grand gets you a warm welcome, a regular’s order and, it seems, a reputation as the café’s most persistent mild critic. The app doesn’t know who it’s talking to. It only knows a finger landed somewhere and somewhere was worth three stars.

There’s a meaner way to put it, too. Every one of those reviews dragged down the score of a place I love, in my name, with my money still warm in their till. I have effectively been paying them to damage their own rating. That isn’t loyalty. That’s a protection racket in reverse.

### What I’ve asked for

So, I wrote to them. Politely, because I like them, and at length, because I’m me. The email bounced, naturally. The food arrives every single time; the complaint couldn’t find the door.

Once it got through, I asked them to take it to whoever makes the app and get them to:

- test the review prompt on a real iPhone with VoiceOver running, and write down what it actually announces;

- explain how a review gets submitted: whether closing the prompt counts, whether a rating is pre-selected, and whether anything confirms it was sent;

- give every control a proper name, and give me a “Not now” that genuinely means not now;

- find the reviews attributed to me and remove the ones I never meant, rather than quietly swapping them for five stars, which would just be a nicer lie;

- tell me whether blind people ever test their releases, and whether this same prompt is sitting in other restaurant apps doing the same thing to other people.

That last one is the bit that bothers me most. If it’s a shared component, there are blind customers all over the place leaving reviews they never wrote, and small cafés wondering why their regulars seem so unimpressed.

### For the record

I don’t want my reviews changed. I want them never to have existed. When I think somewhere deserves three stars, I’ll say so myself. I’m perfectly capable. I’ve had two thousand pounds’ worth of practice deciding what I like.

The food: five stars. The service: five stars. The people: five stars. The button: we’ll see.
