## Monday, 06:10

The Trust said yes.

Gwen Hale, the chair, had rung rather than written. She said she had read the unknowns list twice, the second time out loud to the trustees, and that the mailbox from 2019 had belonged to a volunteer called Pat, who had died, and whom nobody had had the heart to switch off.

"We don't want a pilot," Gwen said. "We want the whole thing. But we've had audits before. We had one in 2021. It was a hundred and forty pages long and it made us feel terrible, and we fixed about a third of it."

"I'm not going to audit you," Ruth said. "I'm going to build you a new one next to the old one. When it's ready, your people test it. If it does everything the old one did, you switch. If it doesn't, you don't."

"And what do we fix?"

"Nothing," said Ruth. "That's the point."

So on Monday morning she made tea, put her earpiece in and woke the swarm with a brief she had been rewriting in her head for a fortnight.

> brief: the Merrow Trust, full remake, with consent
>
> old site: evidence, not blueprint
>
> new site: accessible by construction
>
> switchover test: nothing lost
>
> modeller: Ruth Ashworth (human)
>
> key holder: Gwen Hale (human)

She had added the last line herself, too. She was the one making it. She would never be the one who owned it.

## Monday, 09:30

The observers had done this before, and it showed. They didn't send her four hundred and twelve pages this time. They sent her a ledger.

> facts found on the old site: 2,904
>
> facts duplicated: 611
>
> facts contradicting each other: 38
>
> forms: 37, doing 9 different things
>
> PDFs: 64, carrying 212 facts that appear nowhere else

Every opening time, phone number, eligibility rule, course date, fee, name and promise on the old site had gone into one list, each one tagged with every page it came from. It wasn't a site map. It was a list of everything the Trust had ever told the public, and it was the thing the new site would be measured against.

Ruth called it the manifest. She had called it something else in 1989, but she couldn't remember what, and the swarm liked the word.

## Tuesday to Thursday

The factory ran.

There was no other word for it. Analysts took facts from the manifest and filed them under the five intentions. Builders made pages, each one starting from what someone had come to do, not from what the old menu said. Every page was built from parts that already worked: headings that nested, controls that said what they were, forms that told you what had happened when you sent them. Nobody checked afterwards whether a button had a name. A button couldn't be made without one.

Testers came through in waves, with screen readers, with magnifiers at four hundred per cent, with a single switch, with braille, and as agents carrying intentions from people who didn't exist. When a tester failed, the fault went back down the line to the part that made it, and every page made from that part was fixed at once. Ruth listened to the failures, not the passes. Passes told you nothing.

On Wednesday the builders proposed a carousel for the home page. Ruth said no without waiting for the reasoning. On Thursday they proposed it again, with better reasoning. She said no again, and wrote the reason into the model so they would stop.

## Friday, 11:00

Then came the test that mattered.

The swarm read the old site and the new site side by side, fact by fact, against the manifest. Not "does it pass". Does it say the same thing. Is every fact that the old site gave a sighted reader now available to everyone, by whatever means they use?

> facts carried: 2,861
>
> facts merged (duplicates): 611, every source page redirected
>
> contradictions resolved: 38, each one confirmed by the Trust
>
> inbound links: 1,180, every one lands
>
> information casualties: 4

Four. Ruth had them read to her one at a time.

> casualty 1: old site, /contact: "email pat@merrowtrust.org.uk". Not on new site.

That one was meant. She marked it retired, and wrote the reason in Gwen's words.

> casualty 2: old site, home page: a photograph of the 1998 summer trip to Whitby, with no description. Not on new site.

She stopped at that one. A picture with no description had told sighted visitors something for years and told her nothing at all. It was the only fact on the old site she had never had. It would be a poor sort of remake that dropped it now. She asked Gwen who was in it. Gwen asked Dev, who had been there. The photograph went onto the About page, with three sentences about a windy day, a lost hat and the Abbey steps.

The other two were course dates that had already passed. Retired, with reasons.

> information casualties: 0 unexplained

The new site had passed. Or more truthfully, there was now nothing for it to fail.

## The following Tuesday

Gwen brought the staff into the Trust's training room. Dev Mistry, who taught the employability course and had used JAWS since he was nineteen, sat at the front with the old site on one laptop and the new one on another, and did what he did every day with his students. He tried to apply for a course.

On the old site it took him eleven minutes, and he didn't know at the end whether it had worked.

On the new site it took three. At the end it said, "We've received your application. Your reference is MT-4471. It's waiting for someone to review it. We'll tell you when that changes."

"Well," said Dev, "that's new."

They tested for two days. They found nine things, and the factory fixed them in the time it took Gwen to make a round of coffee. On Thursday afternoon Ruth sent Gwen the summary: what had changed, what had been retired and why, the casualty list at zero, Dev's nine findings and their fixes, and one line at the bottom.

> Reply "switch" to put the new site live. The old one becomes a map that sends every old link to the right new page.

Gwen replied at 16:52.

> switch

## Friday, 06:00

The new site was eighteen hours old when the first report arrived.

It came from the link at the foot of the course page, "Fix something on this page", which Ruth had insisted on and Gwen had worried about. ("What if people use it to complain?" "They will. That's what it's for.")

> What happened: the course dates are read as numbers with slashes. I heard "one slash four slash twenty-seven" and had to work out which way round it was.
>
> What you expected: just say the date.
>
> How were you reading: braille display, and VoiceOver on the Mac.

Nobody had asked for a name, and nobody had one. The reporter got a private link to their report instead, and the advice to bookmark it.

At six o'clock Phix woke, found the report waiting and claimed it. It reproduced the problem the way the reporter had met it, with braille and VoiceOver. It found that the course dates were coming in from the booking system in a format nobody had told the factory about. It fixed the cause, not the page, so every date on the site would now read as "1 April 2027". It added a test so the slashes could never come back, and put the fixed site on a preview address of its own.

Then it stopped, because the next step wasn't its to take.

At 07:15 Gwen's phone gave her an email with a link that worked once. She opened it on the bus, read what had been reported, what Phix understood and what it had changed, and pressed **Publish**.

At 07:40 the reporter's private page said: "Does it work for you now?"

At 08:03, from somewhere in the north, on a braille display:

> works for me

## Friday, 15:00

Ruth listened to the whole thing in the order it had happened, from the report to the reply, at her kitchen table, with her tea.

In 1989 a project had ended at go-live. You handed over the system, the manual and the bill, and by the time anyone found what you had got wrong you were three clients away. That was why testing had been a separate job, done at the end by somebody else, and why its reports were so long. It was the last chance anybody had.

That wasn't how it worked any more. Go-live was where it started. The site would be kept by three agents she had never met. Cloud Dancer would take Gwen's wishes for new pages and hold them on a preview until Gwen said yes. Flo would put the news up when Gwen asked. Phix would wake at six and noon and listen to whoever was hindered. None of them could take the house. Gwen had the key.

And the testing, the job she had watched being done last on every project of her career, hadn't gone away. It had moved. It now belonged to whoever used the site, someone on a braille display who had pressed a link and been listened to, and it would go on as long as the site did.

Ruth closed the session. The factory had nothing left to make. It had never been the clever part.

The site wasn't finished, and it never would be. For the first time in her working life, that was the good news.
