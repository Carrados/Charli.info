# Dr Adaeze

> Dr Adaeze is the Historian of the Museum Court, who insists that the history of assistive technology is a history of lives, not inventors.

**Canon.** This entry is written from inside the CharliVerse, so its people and companies are fictional. Behind the scenes says what is real.

**Dr Adaeze** is the Historian of the [Museum Court](https://charli.info/charlipedia/the-museum-court/). Dark curls, grey beginning at the temples. A PhD, and the person in the room who has read everything and forgotten nothing.

## Lives, not inventors

She doesn't chronicle products; she interprets them. Her question is not what happened, but what it means that it happened. The argument she most wants the museum to make is one made nowhere else: the history of assistive technology is a history of lives, not inventors. Most museums celebrate the person who invented Braille. This one celebrates the moment a woman could finally read a letter in private.

## Against triumph

She is suspicious of stories about inevitable progress. The [Hall of Broken Promises](https://charli.info/charlipedia/hall-of-broken-promises/) exists because she insisted on it. Most assistive technology doesn't fail for lack of innovation; it fails because it misjudges lived constraint, the real street, the real budget, the real fatigue. The people who built it weren't malicious. They were disconnected from the real environment. She won't let those failures be softened into lessons learned. They are evidence.

## Sideways, and backwards

She treats the [sideways breakthrough](https://charli.info/charlipedia/sideways-breakthroughs/) as a historical category, not an exception, and holds the policy layer too: institutions change the structure of opportunity even when the technology stays the same. She also records regression. The move from the plain-text web of the late 1990s to the image-heavy, script-laden web of the 2010s was a step backwards in access. Accessibility history is not a smooth curve; it is a patchwork of advances and stagnations.

## With the Court

Her distance from personal history lets her see a structural pattern where a depositor sees only their own exhaustion. That isn't coldness; it's the function the museum needs. [Tamsin](https://charli.info/charlipedia/tamsin/) makes her arguments liveable. She would leave if the museum became a History of Progress, because without the negative space of failure it would be a PR agency for the technology industry.
