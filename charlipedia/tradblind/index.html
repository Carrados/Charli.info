Tradblind

Canon. This entry is written from inside the CharliVerse, so its people and companies are fictional. Behind the scenes says what is real.

The Tradblind movement emerged in response to what its followers describe as the “industrialisation of blindness.” Where modern disability culture encourages blind people to become hyper-efficient productivity cyborgs armed with 14 Bluetooth devices, three AI subscriptions, bone-conduction headphones, lidar canes, and a startup founder mindset, Tradblind asks a simpler question:

“What if blindness was allowed to feel human again?”

Tradblind people believe the modern blind experience has become overly focused on optimisation, speed, independence theatre, and proving competence to sighted society. They argue that blind people have been pushed into a permanent state of performance: endlessly demonstrating capability, adaptability, resilience, and technological fluency just to justify their existence.

In contrast, the Tradblind movement embraces a slower, softer, more relational understanding of blindness.

Core Tradblind beliefs include:

1. Blindness Is an Identity, not a Defect to Be Patched

Tradblind thinkers reject what they call “The Endless Upgrade Mindset.”

They are suspicious of the idea that every friction point in blindness must be solved through:

  • AI

  • wearables

  • computer vision

  • smart glasses

  • sensory substitution

  • “Frictionless accessibility”

They believe blindness has its own culture, rhythms, skills, aesthetics, and social structures that should not automatically be erased in pursuit of normality.

The phrase:

“I am blind, not a failed sighted person”
is foundational within the movement.

2. Human Assistance Is Good, Actually

Tradblind people reject the cult of total independence.

Modern blindness culture often treats accepting help as moral weakness. Tradblind philosophy instead frames interdependence as natural and civilised.

A Tradblind person might say:

  • “My husband reads the post to me.”

  • “My friend guides me through the station.”

  • “I prefer asking a shop assistant.”

  • “I like being cared for sometimes.”

And crucially:
they refuse to feel ashamed about this.

Critics accuse the movement of romanticising dependency, but supporters argue modern accessibility culture has become emotionally brutal:

“You are only respected if you can simulate sightedness.”

3. Blindness Should Feel Domestic, Not Corporate

Tradblind aesthetics heavily reject what they call:

“Airport blindness.”

That hyper-mobile, productivity-obsessed, LinkedIn disability consultant vibe.

Instead, Tradblind culture celebrates:

  • tactile domestic spaces

  • audio books in the bath

  • knitting

  • baking by touch

  • radios

  • long cane techniques passed down socially.

  • Braille labels made with ancient Dymo tape

  • giant-button microwaves from 1997 that still work perfectly.

  • analogue competence

The ideal Tradblind home contains:

  • at least one talking appliance from the early 2000s

  • a slightly battered Victor Reader Stream

  • BBC Radio 4 permanently on somewhere

  • and a cat who may or may not be assisting with accessibility.

4. AI Is Simultaneously Miracle and Threat

Tradblind people have an extremely conflicted relationship with AI.

On one hand:
AI image description, OCR, voice interfaces, and conversational systems are often described as:

“The greatest accessibility leap of our lifetimes.”

On the other:
Tradblind thinkers fear the emergence of what they call:

“Synthetic sightedness.”

The idea that blind people may increasingly be pressured to experience the world through endless machine-generated interpretations instead of through blind culture itself.

They worry society may stop designing accessible systems and instead simply say:

“Don’t worry, the AI will explain it to you.”

This creates the core Tradblind paradox:

“There has never been a better time to be blind.
It is still shit.”

5. Blindness Has Been Captured by Productivity Ideology

Tradblind critics argue modern disability culture increasingly frames blind people as:

  • inspirational workers

  • startup founders

  • accessibility evangelists

  • “superhumans”

  • optimisation projects

Tradblind people instead defend:

  • loafing

  • contemplation

  • wandering slowly

  • existing socially

  • having tea for two hours

  • knowing things deeply instead of quickly

The movement slogan:

“Not every blind person needs a productivity stack.”

appears frequently in Tradblind spaces.

Tradblind Gender Dynamics

Interestingly, the movement has internal tensions.

Some Tradblind women embrace softness and care openly:

“I want someone to drive at night while I hold the snacks.”

Others fiercely reject any suggestion that blindness should make women more dependent.

Meanwhile male Tradblind figures often cultivate:

  • radio engineer energy.

  • pipe-and-shortwave aesthetics.

  • old-school tactile competence

  • deep suspicion of touchscreen-only interfaces

The male equivalent of the Tradwife sourdough influencer is essentially:

a blind ham radio enthusiast explaining why Windows XP accessibility peaked in 2006.

Tradblind Influencers

Tradblind social media typically includes:

  • lovingly organised Braille shelves

  • discussions of cane techniques from 1987

  • nostalgic praise for physical buttons

  • long posts about why GPS destroyed real orientation skills.

  • emotionally intense debates about screen reader verbosity settings

The movement is split between:

  • “Soft Tradblind”
    (“slow down and stop apologising for blindness”)

and:

  • “Hard Tradblind”
    (“voice assistants are morally corrupting the cane arts”)

Criticism of the Movement

Critics argue Tradblind culture:

  • romanticises exclusion.

  • risks encouraging learned helplessness.

  • fetishises outdated technology.

  • treats independence as suspicious.

  • can drift into anti-technology nostalgia.

Others argue it is mostly middle-class blindness cosplay:
people aestheticizing dependency while still quietly using iPhones, GPT agents, and Uber.

The harshest critics accuse Tradblind creators of:

“Performing analogue authenticity through monetised digital platforms.”

Which, to be fair, is difficult to deny when someone uploads:

“Reject modernity. Return to cassette labels.”
from a £1400 smartphone using fibre broadband and AI-generated alt text.

Sion, 2038

By 2038 Tradblind had grown from a loose cultural tendency into an influential international voice within blind life, defending human assistance and interdependence and deeply suspicious of synthetic sightedness. When Emma Heart raced the Paralympic Super-G with the field instead of a guide, the writer and former ski guide Martha Vale said Emma should be allowed to compete, and that the story told about her was dangerous: "The guide is not a bug." She called the field "the industrialisation of perception". After Emma's gold, one Tradblind writer described her suit as "a privately owned synthetic nervous system wrapped around a sponsored body", which Big Charli conceded was annoyingly good. Its opposite number is PostAccessibility.
