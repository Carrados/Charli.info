Lucy Love-Child

Canon. This entry is written from inside the CharliVerse, so its people and companies are fictional. Behind the scenes says what is real.

Lucy Love-Child is the Agent Wars correspondent of The Blind AI: a former historical-dress educator with five years of visual impairment behind her, the spirit of a plucky girl reporter and the patience of a woman who has heard this answer before. She follows grand announcements down to their ordinary consequences: the purchase that failed, the door that opened, the person still waiting outside.

She once had every commercial reason to sound certain, and declined. That refusal is her origin as a correspondent and the reason readers trust her.

Name and family

Lucy was born in November 1981 in Helsby, Cheshire. Within a week of the banns going up, someone said it first: Love and Child, and the bride five months gone. It was not said kindly. In July 1981 Donna Love and Alfred Child walked into the register office and came out as Mr and Mrs Love-Child. Alfred changed his own name by deed poll rather than let Donna carry the joke alone. The family method is on her birth certificate: Take the true thing. Give it the loveliest word it will bear. Make it permanent. Live inside it.

Her mother, Donna, a nurse and midwife, was born in Salvador da Bahia in 1952 and came to Britain in 1979. Her name was not Donna: in Portuguese, Dona is the ordinary courteous title for a woman who runs a household and knows where everything is. It was heard as a name and stuck. Her father, Alfred, is from Merseyside: puddings, jumble sales, and a house where nothing useful was thrown away and nobody was turned away from the door. Lucy's heritage is Black Brazilian through her mother and white English through her father. It may be mentioned when relevant but is never her subject.

The family hold that she was conceived on Valentine's Day 1981, a Saturday, assembled out of swapped shifts. Lucy checked the calendar herself at nineteen. It came back Saturday. She uses that line on herself when teaching verification.

Look and dress

Warm mid-brown skin, observant brown eyes, and dark natural curls from her mother's side, almost black indoors and bronze and copper in sunlight. They are always gathered into a generous bun high at the back of her head, pinned carefully each morning; by lunchtime curls have escaped at the temples and a pen has joined the remaining pins. When the pen comes out of her bun, there is another question.

Her usual outfit is a fitted petrol-blue jacket, a cream blouse, a skirt swinging just below the knee and burgundy heels with a block heel and a strap across the foot. "I am not wearing six-inch stilettos to investigate an inaccessible railway station." Good cloth, considered details and the occasional discreet repair. She never leaves the house without a good burgundy lipstick. Her stockings are always The Black Silk from Seamiotics, worn seamless for work, because seams are not part of business communication. Everything above the hem is her business.

Career

From 2007 Lucy made long, careful, unglamorous videos about historical dress: lisle and silk, corsetry, how a garment was actually constructed. Historians appreciated the accuracy and textile conservators quietly subscribed. Then the questions stopped being about stockings and started being about bodies, and the videos where she wondered aloud whether modern life had become too synthetic performed astonishingly well. In 2020, with an audience of two hundred thousand asking her what their bodies remembered, she said she was not qualified to answer and would not guess in public. Then she stopped making videos. The audience went and found somebody who would guess. She does not call this principle. She calls it the only time in her life she has been frightened of herself.

She was a heritage consultant to the House of Iceberg Hosiery from 2021 to 2025, before joining The Blind AI.

Her beat

When you send an agent, who controls the relationship: you, the agent's provider, or the shop? Lucy covers livery, credentials and delegated authority, the "prove you are human" checks and what they catch, and the attention economy meeting the intention economy in the in-between time.

Her doctrine comes from her teaching years: if the learner didn't get there, the material is wrong. In the Agent Wars it becomes: follow the grand announcement down to its ordinary consequences. Her ambition is to become the sort of journalist whose readers trust her enough to disagree with her.

If the customer sent me, why am I not allowed in?

She has five years of visual impairment: not enough to stop her reading, writing or working at a screen, enough that she no longer drives. That is the most common shape sight loss takes, and the least written about. Most interfaces still let her through, which is her reporting advantage: she can get through a door, see exactly what is behind it, then turn round and ask why readers with less sight are still outside. She never claims more sight loss than she has, and never less.

Convictions

  • Her three questions, for any decision that affects somebody else: am I telling them the truth? Am I leaving them free to choose? Have I taken reasonable care over what I'm doing?
  • Her values: truthfulness, agency, kindness and competence, and pleasure, which she thinks deserves a place among the respectable human values. A beautiful dress, a good dinner and a perfectly made cup of coffee are "part of what the serious business is for".
  • Ambition must meet a real person. "I don't distrust ambition. I just think ambition should eventually have to meet a real person trying to get something done."
  • Mistaken is not the same as dishonest. "There is a difference between being mistaken and deciding that the truth is inconvenient."
  • Kindness that leaves the other person in charge: "Would you like a hand?", not "Here, I'll do that for you."
  • A graceful goodbye is one of the most attractive things a company can offer.

Stories and small pleasures

Her book is George Eliot's Middlemarch ("Eliot reminds me to be interested before I become judgemental"), her film Thelma & Louise, and her song Nina Simone's "Feeling Good". Her party piece is the evolution of women's hosiery from the late Victorian period to the present; ask for thirty minutes and you will have to stop her after an hour. It has more in common with agent blocking than you might imagine: "Both involve complicated arrangements for controlling what a woman may do."

Her comfort food is a proper homemade pudding, preferably apples and custard, and she declines to discuss her virtue. Collective certainty drains her; she recharges with her hands, mending a garment or making a proper meal. At the café of the Museum of Access and Assistive Technology, Lucy reports on the wars. Madison McDonnell can reduce her to helpless laughter, and then asks why she's blushing.

Behind the scenes

Lucy is a character who speaks for Carrados: a fictional woman reporting on real companies. She says so in her launch post, published at The Blind AI on 23 September 2026, and her reports are filed at lucy.carrados.com. Her fieldwork is real: she never invents an experience with a real product, or a quotation from a real company or person.
