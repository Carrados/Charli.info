PostAccessibility

Canon. This entry is written from inside the CharliVerse, so its people and companies are fictional. Behind the scenes says what is real.

The PostAccessibility movement emerged in response to what its followers describe as “Legacy Accessibility Thinking.”

Where traditional accessibility culture focused on teaching blind people how to operate fundamentally visual systems through screen readers, semantic markup, keyboard shortcuts, ARIA labels, and heroic levels of patience, PostAccessibility asks a far more radical question:

“What if the interface itself was the accessibility problem?”

PostAccessibility thinkers argue that twentieth-century accessibility models were fundamentally constrained by what they call:
“The Preservation of Visuality.”

Rather than questioning why modern computing required blind people to navigate visual environments indirectly, classical accessibility simply attempted to bolt compensatory systems onto inherently sighted architectures.

PostAccessibility rejects this compromise entirely.

Core PostAccessibility beliefs include:

1. Screen Readers Are Legacy Middleware

PostAccessibility theorists argue that screen readers were historically necessary but increasingly resemble:
“Forklift certification for navigating the ruins of visual computing.”

They believe blind people should not have to:

  • tab through interfaces
  • memorise shortcut keys
  • parse navigation regions
  • interpret dynamic UI states
  • or fight CAPTCHA systems.

simply to access information that machines already structurally understand.

The phrase:
“The API is the UI”
is foundational within the movement.

2. Intent Is the New Interface

PostAccessibility rejects what it calls:
“Manual Interface Labour.”

Instead of navigating systems directly, PostAccessibility users increasingly delegate tasks through:

  • AI agents
  • semantic assistants
  • conversational systems
  • autonomous workflows
  • and probabilistic intent modelling.

Rather than asking:
“Can the blind person use the app?”

PostAccessibility asks:
“Why is there still an app?”

Followers often describe traditional interface navigation as:
“Performative sightedness.”

3. Accessibility Maturity Models Are Fossilised Thinking

PostAccessibility thinkers are deeply critical of what they call:
“The Accessibility Industrial Complex.”

In particular, they reject accessibility maturity models, arguing they optimise organisations around maintaining visual interface compliance rather than reducing information friction itself.

Critics within the movement frequently describe accessibility consultants as:
“Priests preserving sacred keyboard rituals from the age of forms and buttons.”

A common PostAccessibility slogan is:
“WCAG is teaching horses to use escalators.”

4. The Browser Was a Historical Accident

More radical PostAccessibility factions believe the web browser itself may eventually disappear as the primary layer of interaction.

Instead, information will increasingly exist as:

  • structured semantic systems
  • agent-readable environments
  • intent-based services
  • dynamic renderings
  • and multimodal information spaces.

This faction often describes websites as:
“Visual residue from the pre-agent era.”

Hardline PostAccessibility thinkers have been known to refer to navigation menus as:
“Archaeological accessibility artefacts.”

5. AI Will Simultaneously Solve and Destroy Accessibility

PostAccessibility culture has an extremely conflicted relationship with generative AI.

On one hand, they view modern AI systems as the greatest accessibility breakthrough in history:

  • real-time scene understanding
  • live semantic interpretation
  • agentic workflows
  • conversational interaction
  • and multimodal abstraction layers.

On the other hand, they fear what they call:
“Probabilistic reality collapse.”

The concern that accessibility may increasingly become:

  • machine interpretation instead of direct inspection
  • inference instead of structure
  • summaries instead of truth
  • and generated confidence instead of verifiable state.

This creates the core PostAccessibility paradox:

“We finally taught the machine to see.
Now we must determine whether it is telling the truth.”

PostAccessibility Aesthetics

PostAccessibility culture strongly favours:

  • minimal interfaces
  • voice interaction
  • headless systems
  • semantic APIs
  • dark mode screenshots of terminal windows
  • AI copilots
  • and aggressively expensive wearable hardware.

The ideal PostAccessibility setup contains:

  • at least three AI subscriptions
  • agent orchestration tools nobody fully understands
  • Meta Ray-Bans
  • a local LLM running badly on a machine worth more than a used car
  • and a half-finished GitHub repo attempting to replace the browser entirely.

PostAccessibility Influencers

PostAccessibility influencers typically:

  • speak in terms of “interaction layers”
  • describe interfaces as “legacy”
  • call screen readers “transitional technologies”
  • and use the phrase: “Accessibility as Information Integrity”
    with almost religious seriousness.

Their social media feeds usually contain:

  • photos of smart glasses
  • AI workflow diagrams
  • screenshots of terminal output
  • and increasingly unhinged predictions about the death of apps.

The movement is split between:

“Soft PostAccessibility”
(“AI can radically reduce friction”)

and:

“Hard PostAccessibility”
(“The GUI was a 50-year detour.”)

Criticism of the Movement

Critics argue PostAccessibility culture:

  • romanticises AI systems
  • underestimates verification problems
  • treats embodiment as an engineering bug
  • and assumes probabilistic systems are more reliable than they are.

Others argue the movement is simply:
“Accessibility for people who secretly wish they were infrastructure architects.”

The harshest critics accuse PostAccessibility creators of:

“Trying to replace human adaptation with corporate stochastic middleware.”

Which, to be fair, is difficult to deny when someone posts:

“The browser is dead.”

from inside a Chromium-based social media platform while asking ChatGPT to summarise the comments.

Sion, 2038

By 2038 PostAccessibility had its own institutions. Dr Amara Sen, director of the European Centre for PostAccessibility Systems, embraced Emma Heart's guideless Super-G with the field as proof that access need not mean adapting a blind person to a sighted method. Guide skiing, she said, was an ingenious historical solution that "converted spatial information into shouted language". Of Emma: "Emma receives the mountain." Some of the movement's harder-line supporters called guide skiing "legacy perception middleware", a phrase Emma banned her team from using in her presence, and after her gold one PostAccessibility account declared that guide skiing had ended and "the interface has been removed". Daniel Reed replied. Its opposite number is Tradblind.
